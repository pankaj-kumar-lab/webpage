Research Interests

Research Description

We collaborate with a battery of scientists across grounds whose research interests are varied and span different areas like the microbiome, metagenomics, metabolomics, proteomics, transcriptomics, eQTL analysis, single nucleotide variation, structure variation of the genome, single-cell genomics to understand the heterogeneity between the cells, etc. We have been supporting colleagues and collaborators across the university with bioinformatics analysis, statistical analysis, data interpretation and grant writing (as a Co-I) to fulfill aims that need bioinformatics and statistical expertise.

Our laboratory uses computational methods to make novel biological discoveries about the regulation of gene expression in cancers from genomic and transcriptomic data generated by consortia like The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), Encyclopedia of DNA elements (ENCODE), etc. An unbiased approach to analyzing high-throughput small RNA sequencing data resulted in the discovery of a new class of small RNAs called transfer RNA fragments (tRFs). Ongoing projects in the lab examine how tRFs impact on gene expression and cancer.

Extrachromosomal Circular DNA: Biogenesis, Genomic Instability, and Cancer Evolution

Our research investigates the formation, molecular characteristics, and biological functions of extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA), a class of DNA molecules that exist outside chromosomes and are increasingly recognized for their roles in genome regulation, genomic instability, and cancer.

Our early work contributed to the discovery and characterization of extrachromosomal microDNAs in normal tissues and their association with chromosomal microdeletions. Subsequent studies investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying eccDNA formation, including the involvement of DNA mismatch repair pathways and transcriptional activity. We also demonstrated that normal and cancerous tissues release eccDNA into circulation, suggesting potential applications in understanding disease-associated genomic alterations. Additional collaborative research established that small circular DNAs can generate regulatory RNAs capable of modulating gene expression.

A major focus of our research has been the development of computational approaches for identifying and characterizing eccDNA from high-throughput sequencing data. We demonstrated that ATAC-seq can be used to detect thousands of eccDNA molecules in cancer tissues and cell lines, expanding opportunities to investigate circular DNA using existing genomic datasets.

Our current research focuses on understanding the role of large, oncogene-amplifying extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) in cancer, particularly glioblastoma and pediatric malignancies. By integrating whole-genome sequencing, single-cell ATAC-seq, and single-cell RNA-seq, we aim to identify ecDNA-containing cells, characterize genomic alterations at their chromosomal sites of origin, and investigate how ecDNA is associated with cellular heterogeneity, transcriptional states, and tumor evolution.

A central question is whether the chromosomal loci that generate ecDNA retain identifiable genomic signatures that can provide insights into the mechanisms of ecDNA formation. We are developing computational strategies to examine these signatures at single-cell resolution and connect ecDNA architecture with cancer cell identity and gene regulation.

Our long-term goal is to understand how extrachromosomal DNA contributes to genome instability, oncogene amplification, and cancer evolution, while developing computational tools and analytical frameworks that enable its systematic investigation across cancer types.

Circle_finder

Pankaj Kumar was one of the first authors from the group (postdoc lab) that used first paired-end high throughput sequencing methods to identify circular DNA at base-pair resolution. Pankaj Kumar developed one of the first computational methods (Circle_finder available at Github), which can identify circular DNA from any type of paired-end high throughput sequencing data. The genome-wide abundance of eccDNAs and their functional importance are not very well understood. These eccDNA sequences are a few hundred kbs to a few megabases in size. Pankaj Kumar's interest is in longer eccDNA as they are expected to harbor full-length gene sequences. Pankaj Kumar has been working on circular DNA for over a decade.

Transfer RNA-Derived Fragments: Biogenesis, Gene Regulation, and Cancer Biology

Our research investigates the biogenesis, molecular functions, and biological significance of transfer RNA-derived fragments (tRFs), an emerging class of small noncoding RNAs generated from precursor or mature transfer RNAs. Although transfer RNAs are traditionally known for their essential role in protein synthesis, accumulating evidence demonstrates that tRNA-derived fragments have distinct regulatory functions in gene expression and cellular processes.

Our work has contributed to understanding the evolutionary conservation, molecular characteristics, and regulatory mechanisms of tRFs. Through computational analyses of small RNA sequencing datasets, we identified conserved tRF populations and demonstrated their association with Argonaute (AGO) proteins, suggesting their involvement in sequence-specific post-transcriptional gene regulation. Subsequent studies established that certain tRFs can guide AGO proteins to regulate target gene expression through a Dicer-independent mechanism.

A major focus of our research has been the development of computational resources and predictive approaches to facilitate the systematic investigation of tRF biology. We developed tRFdb (https://genome.bioch.virginia.edu/), a database of tRNA-derived fragments, and contributed to tRForest, a machine learning-based algorithm for predicting tRF target genes. These resources support the identification, characterization, and functional interpretation of tRFs across biological systems.

Our research also explores the functional significance of tRFs in cancer. Recent work investigating tRF-3021a has demonstrated its role in promoting resistance to apoptosis and supporting global protein synthesis in cancer cell lines, highlighting the potential importance of tRF-mediated regulatory mechanisms in cancer cell survival.

Together, these studies advance our understanding of noncanonical tRNA functions and provide a foundation for investigating how tRF-mediated gene regulation contributes to cancer biology and other human diseases.

Precision Cancer Risk Prediction and Prevention Using Genomics and Real-World Clinical Data

Our research focuses on integrating large-scale genomic data, longitudinal electronic health records, and clinical and nonclinical risk factors to advance precision cancer risk prediction and prevention. Using data from the NIH All of Us Research Program, we develop computational and statistical approaches to investigate how inherited genetic susceptibility, clinical characteristics, and medication exposures influence cancer risk. Our long-term goal is to improve individualized cancer risk assessment and identify opportunities for personalized prevention strategies.

Integrating Polygenic Risk Scores and Clinical Factors for Breast Cancer Risk Prediction

We investigate how inherited genetic susceptibility, measured using polygenic risk scores (PRS), contributes to breast cancer risk beyond established clinical and demographic factors. By integrating whole-genome sequencing data with longitudinal electronic health records and social determinants of health, we evaluate the predictive performance of ancestry-informed PRS models across genetically diverse populations.

Our research also examines interactions between genetic susceptibility and clinical risk factors and explores individualized five-year breast cancer risk estimation. This work aims to improve risk stratification and provide a foundation for more personalized breast cancer screening and prevention strategies.

Drug Repurposing and Genomics-Informed Breast Cancer Prevention

Building on our breast cancer risk prediction research, we are investigating whether existing medications prescribed for common chronic conditions are associated with breast cancer incidence and whether these associations vary according to inherited genetic susceptibility.

We integrate longitudinal medication records, clinical information, and whole-genome sequencing data to evaluate potential preventive associations of commonly prescribed medications. Using rigorous epidemiologic and statistical approaches, we seek to identify promising drug candidates and determine whether their associations with breast cancer risk differ across genetically defined populations.

This research aims to establish a framework for genomics-informed drug repurposing and generate evidence to guide future studies of personalized cancer prevention.
